# Real refraction on the web — what exists, what it costs, what I recommend

Written 2026-08-06 for the darling-woo design pass, after Kris said the CSS glass
„doesn't pass off as actual light and refractions and crystal like surfaces".

## The diagnosis, first, because it decides everything else

He is right, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of tuning.

**CSS gradients PAINT A PICTURE of light. They never bend anything.** A gradient can
imitate the *look* of a highlight, but refraction is not a look — it is an operation:
*sample the background at a displaced position*, where the displacement comes from the
surface normal and Snell's law. No amount of layering gradients performs that operation,
so no amount of layering will ever read as glass to an eye that has seen glass.

Exactly three mechanisms on the web can actually displace the backdrop:

1. **SVG `feDisplacementMap` driven by `backdrop-filter: url(#filter)`**
2. **WebGL** (a shader that samples a texture of what is behind)
3. **A pre-baked render** — do the physics offline in a path tracer, ship the result as an image

Everything else in circulation is one of these three wearing a different name.

## 1. SVG displacement — the „liquid glass" route

The technique that the Apple-style clones use: an `<feImage>` loads a pre-computed
displacement map, `<feDisplacementMap>` reads its **red channel as the X offset and green
as the Y offset** (128 = no shift), and `backdrop-filter: url(#id)` applies it to whatever
is behind the element. The map itself is generated by running Snell's law across the
element's surface geometry once, offline or at init.

- **Weight:** tiny — a few KB, and in the best case zero JavaScript at runtime.
- **Support:** *** Chrome only. *** Firefox and Safari do not support SVG filters in
  `backdrop-filter`. There is an open W3C proposal (svgwg issue 1142) to standardise a
  backdrop displacement primitive, and a Mozilla Connect request, but neither is shipped.
- **Real limitation:** the displacement map is computed for a specific size. Changing the
  element's size or shape means rebuilding the map, so it suits **fixed-size components**
  and not fluid ones. Animating only the `scale` parameter is cheap; animating geometry is not.

## 2. WebGL — real physics, but it has to see the page

A shader can do the whole model properly: signed distance field for the shape, a lens
height profile, surface normals, Snell refraction, **dispersion** (refract three times at
slightly different IOR per channel — this is the cue the eye reads as *crystal* rather than
*plastic*), Beer–Lambert absorption so thick parts saturate, Fresnel so the rim brightens
where the surface turns away, and caustics focused inside.

- **Weight:** the prototype in `glass-webgl.html` is **~4 KB of hand-written shader and
  glue, zero libraries.** With three.js it would be ~600 KB; with a minimal helper like
  twgl or ogl, ~30–50 KB. None of that is necessary for one element.
- **Support:** everywhere.
- *** The real cost is not the shader. It is that WebGL cannot see the DOM. *** The page
  behind the glass must be captured into a texture. The published libraries (LiquidGlass,
  the react-three-fiber transmission materials) do this with `html-to-image` and openly
  report **100–500 ms initialisation**, plus a re-capture on every resize — and, in
  practice, on every scroll if the backdrop moves. For a storefront with a 60-second
  micro-cache and category pages full of cards, that is a large bill for one button.

## 3. Pre-baked render — the physics happens once, offline

Render the actual object in a path tracer (Blender/Cycles), with real glass, real caustics
and real dispersion, and export a WebP.

- **Weight:** 15–40 KB, one image. Nothing at runtime.
- **Support:** everywhere, including the weakest phone.
- **Limitation:** fixed geometry. One size, one background. A 9-slice or a small set of
  sizes covers a button; it does not cover arbitrary layouts.
- **Quality:** *** unmatched. A path tracer does in four seconds offline what the shader
  approximates in 16 milliseconds per frame. ***

## What I recommend, and why

**For the hero button, the badge and the bow: bake it.** They are few, they are a fixed
size, and they are exactly where quality matters. The offline render will look better than
any runtime approximation, and on the site it costs one image.

**WebGL earns its keep only if the refraction must respond to the content behind it in
real time.** On this site it would not — the hero band is a flat claret field. Paying
100–500 ms of DOM capture plus a live canvas for an effect over a solid colour is the kind
of trade this design pass has refused all the way through.

**The SVG route is the one to revisit if it ever standardises.** It is the cheapest of the
three and the most CSS-native, and it is currently blocked only by Chrome being alone.

## The prototype

`glass-webgl.html` renders the real hero band as a texture and lays a glass slab across the
headline, so the type visibly bends at its edge. It is there to answer one question before
any of the cost questions matter: **does actual refraction read as glass where the CSS did
not?** If yes, the remaining question is only delivery. If no, the whole direction can be
dropped cheaply.
